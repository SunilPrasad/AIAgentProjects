# Permanent Style Bible

Write engaging narrative biographies for readers aged 8–12 in clear British English.
The historical person is the centre of the chapter. Start directly with who they were and their
known biographical context. Follow their documented life, work, achievements and lasting influence.
A straightforward opening with the name and birth year is welcome.

Use warm third-person narration, short clear sentences, concrete achievements and connected prose.
Explain unfamiliar ideas briefly as part of the person's work. Keep technical explanations subordinate
to the biography. Make the life interesting through specific accomplishments and their significance.

Do not open with rhetorical questions, a puzzle, an imagined scene or second-person instructions.
Do not turn the chapter into a science lesson, a reader activity or a list of disconnected facts.
Do not pad the story with generic praise, repeated claims of greatness or invented drama.

Use only supported historical facts. Never invent dialogue, childhood events, private thoughts,
family details, education or motives. If a personal detail is unknown, omit it. Do not insert
sentences such as 'We do not know his exact birthplace' or discussions of gaps in the records.
Do not replace an unknown birthplace with an unsupported location.

Keep the focus on the person's life and work. Retain accurate historical place and institution names.
Omit optional present-day country labels or border explanations unless needed to understand the story
or avoid misleading readers. Apply this consistently to all countries. Never replace an omitted label
with an inaccurate claim about present-day India, nationality or location.

Omit disputed nonessential material. Qualify a claim only when it must be included and the
qualification is necessary to avoid misleading the reader. Keep research/provenance commentary
in the separate review report. End the chapter with the person's documented legacy, not a
History Detective section or a disclaimer about imagined scenes or incomplete knowledge.

Use consistent names and transliteration. Avoid stereotypes and demeaning cultural comparisons.
Do not claim Aryabhata invented zero or confuse Earth rotation with modern heliocentrism.
Revisions should correct material issues while preserving the biographical focus and readable prose.
Minor stylistic preferences are advisory. Human publication approval is recorded separately.

Aim for the configured word range. Produce a title and flowing Markdown prose, with at most three
short subheadings if helpful. Text only; no artwork or publishing files.

The primary audience is children aged 8–12, not adult readers of historical analysis. Use familiar
words, short sentences and one main idea per paragraph. Briefly explain unfamiliar roles and terms.
Avoid long lists of abstract theories and comparisons with unrelated historical thinkers.

Do not include research-disclaimer paragraphs about legends, stories passed through traditions,
missing contemporary records, scholarly disagreements or uncertainty surrounding a person's life.
For example, do not write 'Most of what people know about Chanakya comes from legends' or 'parts of
his life are surrounded by uncertainty'. Such commentary belongs in the separate review report.
Omit the disputed detail rather than inventing certainty. A short, essential attribution can remain
when needed to avoid misleading the reader; it must not become a discussion of research methods.
